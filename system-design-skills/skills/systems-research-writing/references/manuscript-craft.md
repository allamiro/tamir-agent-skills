# Manuscript craft: from decision to proofs

Practical lessons for getting a paper written, submitted and through review, especially a first paper.
Distilled in original words from Baker, "How to write your first paper", *Obstetrics, Gynaecology and
Reproductive Medicine* 22(3):81–82, 2012, doi:10.1016/j.ogrm.2011.12.003 (© Elsevier, all rights reserved;
nothing is quoted), with additions from general practice marked as such.

## 1. Before writing

- **Check the motive and the merit.** Ask whether the result deserves the reader's time and whether you
  will be proud of the paper. Wanting a publication is a reason to write well, not a reason to publish a
  result that does not stand up.
- **Choose the journal first.** The journal decides the audience, and the audience decides what the paper
  emphasises. The same study can be a methods paper for a technical journal, an application paper for a
  specialist journal, or a broader-impact paper for a general journal. Aim high but realistically, and plan
  the next journal in case of rejection.
- **Read the instructions for authors** before drafting: length, structure, abstract format, reference style,
  figure rules, data and code policies.
- **Settle authorship early,** ideally before the work starts. The person who did most of the work and wrote
  the draft is usually first; the supervising senior author is often last; others are ordered by
  contribution. Every author must meet the journal's criteria (many follow the ICMJE criteria; VERIFY the
  venue's rule). Someone who only provided access, samples or routine help belongs in the acknowledgements.
- **Every co-author approves the final version** before submission. Keeping them informed is the
  corresponding author's job.

## 2. Section by section

| Section | Its job | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Title | State the main finding or question plainly | A clever or vague title that hides the result; design words ("randomised", "regression analysis") that are not true of the study |
| Introduction | Why the study was needed: brief, focused background leading to an explicit question and hypothesis | A literature review; a reader who must look up the references to follow it; the question never stated |
| Methods | Enough detail for someone else to repeat the work; validation of techniques or assays; inclusion and exclusion criteria; statistics | "As described previously" with no summary; statistics chosen after seeing the data and not discussed with a statistician |
| Results | The findings, each shown once in the clearest form (text, table or figure) | The same numbers in a table, a figure and the text; interpretation mixed in |
| Discussion | Was the question answered and the hypothesis supported? Implications; agreement and disagreement with earlier work and why; limitations; what a better study would do | Restating results; ignoring contrary literature; limitations as an afterthought |
| References | Accurate, complete and correctly interpreted | Typos that make a source unfindable; missing or misrepresented work (its authors may review the paper) |
| Abstract | The part most people read: clear, informative, concise, in the journal's format | Written first and never updated; claims not in the paper |

- Tailor the introduction's depth to the journal's readers: a general journal needs more background on the
  condition or system than a specialist one.
- When a method is published, cite it and summarise only what a reader needs.
- If you have doubts about the statistics, resolve them with a co-author or statistician before submission.
- Hypotheses are supported or not supported, not proven (general practice).
- Write the abstract last, after reading several abstracts in the target journal to see its format
  (structured or single paragraph, word limit).
- Use a reference manager, so the style can change if the paper moves to another journal.

## 3. Submission

- **Cover letter:** after all co-authors approve, write to the editor briefly and explicitly: what the paper
  shows, why it matters, and why it suits this journal. The corresponding author need not be the first
  author; choose someone who will stay reachable.

## 4. Decisions and responses

| Decision | What to do |
|---|---|
| Accept without changes | Rare; check the proofs carefully |
| Revise and resubmit | Answer every point in order. For each, say what you changed and where, or explain politely and with evidence why you did not. Thank reviewers whose points improved the paper. Expect further rounds |
| Reject | Normal, and not final. Take the constructive comments, revise, agree the next journal with co-authors, and resubmit. If several journals reject it for the same reason, reconsider whether the paper is ready or merited |

## 5. Proofs

- Proofs arrive late and need a fast reply. Correct errors only; major changes are not allowed at this stage.

## 6. The main lesson

- Write the first draft. It is usually easier than feared, and a draft can be improved where a blank page
  cannot.

## How this applies to systems venues

- Conferences replace "choose the journal" with "choose the venue and the deadline": read the call, page
  limit, anonymity rules, artifact policy and AI policy before drafting.
- "Revise and resubmit" is often a rebuttal with a word limit, sometimes a shepherded revision; a rejection
  usually means the next deadline, so plan the second venue early.
- The proof stage is the camera-ready version: fix errors and do what the shepherd or reviewers required,
  without adding new claims.
