# Measurement and interpretation

Lessons from quantitative research methods that apply to omics and lab studies. They are paraphrased from
Teo (Ed.), *Handbook of Quantitative Methods for Educational Research*, Sense 2013,
doi:10.1007/978-94-6209-404-8: Hattie's foreword and Wilson and Gochyyev's chapter on psychometrics
(pp. 3–30, doi:10.1007/978-94-6209-404-8_1). They are carried over to biology by analogy and nothing is
quoted, so VERIFY each for your setting.

## Define what a readout measures before collecting it

- Write what low, middle and high levels of the property look like (a construct map). "Pathway activity",
  "stemness", "inflammation score" and "response" are constructs, not observations.
- Choose readouts whose range covers the levels you care about, and say which level each one targets.
- Decide the scoring and categories in advance: finite, exhaustive and ordered, with rules a second
  person could apply the same way.
- Pilot the readout and look at how it behaves. The analogue of a think-aloud session is a run with known
  positive and negative samples.

## Reliability is not validity

- Agreement between replicates or markers shows consistency, not that the readout measures the intended
  property.
- A composite index whose parts define it (formative) need not have correlated parts, so internal
  consistency is the wrong check for it.
- Validity is argued from several kinds of evidence: content, response process, internal structure,
  relations to other variables, and the consequences of using the score.

## Missing values have reasons

- "Not measured", "below detection" and "failed run" are different. Code them differently, and analyse
  why values are missing before you impute.

## Fit the analysis to the design

- Cells nested in patients, or repeated measures over time, need multilevel or longitudinal models.
  Treating them as independent overstates precision.
- Report effect sizes with intervals, and read conventional thresholds (small, medium, large) in context.

## Interpret with consequences in view

- Ask whether the method fitted the question, whether the analysis decisions were reported and sound, whether
  the interpretation follows from the evidence, and whether the data were good enough. Then say what
  decision the result supports and what happens if it is wrong.
